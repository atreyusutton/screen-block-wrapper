# Reels Blockers Replace Instagram, Never Edit It

No app on an ordinary iPhone can reach inside Instagram, TikTok or YouTube and delete the Reels tab, and every product advertised as "removing Reels from Instagram" is quietly doing something else. iOS runs each app in a **sandbox**, a sealed container that cannot see or change any other app. That means Apple's only official tool for controlling other apps, the **Screen Time API**, can cover a whole app with a "blocked" screen but cannot touch a single button inside it, and AppBlock, Opal and one sec all say so publicly ([AppBlock](https://appblock.app/can-appblock-block-instagram-reels-youtube-shorts-or-snapchat-stories-on-iphone/); [Opal Community](https://community.opal.so/t/i-would-like-to-be-able-to-block-a-feature-of-an-app-but-not-the-whole-app-ie-block-reels-but-not-the-whole-of-instagram/3783); [one sec](https://tutorials.one-sec.app/en/articles/4071170)). The Reels-removal products instead do one of four things: show you Instagram's *website* inside their own built-in browser with Reels hidden, hide Reels on the website in Safari through an extension, use an Apple Shortcuts automation to bounce you from the Instagram icon into that filtered website, or record your screen nonstop and kick you out when a Reel appears ([Dull](https://getdull.app/block-reels-iphone/); [ScrollGuard](https://scrollguard.app/blog/how-to-block-instagram-reels-iphone); [WallHabit reviews](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/wallhabit-block-shorts-reels/id6751423279?see-all=reviews&platform=iphone)). The earlier AI advice your team got was therefore half right. You cannot personalize the real apps, but your core concept is fully supported by Apple's tools: block chosen apps, unlock one only after an AI hears a good reason, re-lock it automatically, and check in afterward. Simpler versions already sell on the App Store, such as Binky Block ([Binky Block](https://binkyblock.app/)). The personalization you want is real too, but only inside a filtered "clean room" your app hosts, where each user can switch off Reels, Explore or Shorts on the sites you write filters for; TikTok's and Snapchat's websites expose too little to filter usefully ([UNDOOMED](https://apps.apple.com/app/id6751837079)). The strongest plan is to build the AI gate on Screen Time first, add the filtered web view second, and treat screen-watching as an optional stretch goal. You will need a Mac, a real iPhone, a paid Apple Developer account, and a special Apple approval to publish that has taken a month or more, so a working class demo is very realistic, while an App Store release is possible but carries terms-of-service risk for the web-filtering part.

## iOS seals every app in its own box, so blockers can cover apps but never edit them

The earlier answers your team got blurred two different claims. "iOS won't let your app change other apps" is true. "iOS blocks your design" is false. Separating them starts with the sandbox. Every iPhone app runs sealed off from every other app's screen, memory and files, and Apple gives outside developers no way to look inside another app. Android is different: it offers an **Accessibility Service**, a permission originally built for tools like screen readers, which lets an app read other apps' on-screen layout in real time and press "back" when a Reels screen appears. Google Play allows this for non-accessibility apps that disclose it and get the user's consent ([Google Play Console Help](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/10964491)), and open-source Android blockers detect Shorts and Reels exactly this way ([GitHub: Shorts-Blocker](https://github.com/atick-faisal/Shorts-Blocker)). That gap explains some confusing ads. one sec's "Remove In-App Distractions" feature exists only on Android, and its developer says there is "no way technically to build such a feature on iOS" ([one sec](https://tutorials.one-sec.app/en/articles/4071170)). Fluid Friction similarly works inside Instagram on Android but uses its own browser on iPhone ([Fluid Friction](https://fluidfriction.app/blog/app-to-block-instagram-reels)).

### The Screen Time API locks whole apps, categories and websites

The one official doorway is Apple's **Screen Time API**, introduced with iOS 15 in 2021. An *API* (application programming interface) is a set of functions Apple lets developers call, and this one is split into *frameworks*, which are code libraries with separate jobs. **FamilyControls** asks permission and shows Apple's app picker, **ManagedSettings** applies the blocks, and **DeviceActivity** runs schedules and usage timers ([WWDC Notes](https://wwdcnotes.com/notes/wwdc21/10123)). Since iOS 16 a person can authorize the API for their own phone, confirming with Face ID or Touch ID, instead of needing a parent's approval ([Apple Docs: FamilyControlsMember](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/familycontrols/familycontrolsmember); [Apple Docs: Family Controls](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/familycontrols)). With that permission an app can place a **shield**, Apple's full-screen "this app is blocked" cover, over individual apps, whole categories such as Social, or web domains. It can also flip the logic and block everything *except* a list of up to 50 apps ([Apple Docs: ManagedSettingsStore](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/managedsettings/managedsettingsstore); [Apple Docs: all(except:)](https://developer.apple.com/tutorials/data/documentation/managedsettings/shieldsettings/activitycategorypolicy/all%28except:%29.md)). Two practical limits come from developer reports rather than Apple's docs. A list of more than 50 individually picked apps reportedly blocks nothing at all ([Apple Forums 733361](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/733361)). Some system apps, such as Phone and FaceTime, stay unblocked even under an "all apps" shield ([Apple Forums 729891](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/729891)).

The API is deliberately blindfolded. When the user picks apps in Apple's **FamilyActivityPicker**, your app gets back *tokens*: sealed ID tags that mean "app A" without revealing that app A is Instagram ([Apple Docs: FamilyActivityPicker](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/familycontrols/familyactivitypicker)). Your app can show the app's real name and icon on screen through Apple-drawn labels, but your code, and therefore your AI, cannot read them ([Apple Docs: Family Controls](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/familycontrols); [dev.to, Willheim](https://dev.to/twillywill/the-ios-screen-time-api-in-2026-what-apples-docs-dont-tell-you-151e)). The only exception arrived in iOS 26.4 and works only for users located in the EU, who can grant an app access to real app identifiers ([Apple Docs: FamilyActivityData](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/familycontrols/familyactivitydata)). Saved tokens can also expire on iOS 26.5 and later, so the app must listen for Apple's expiry message and refresh them ([Apple Docs: TokenExpiryMessage](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/managedsettings/managedsettingsstore/tokenexpirymessage)).

### The block screen is a poster, so the AI chat must live in your app

You can customize the shield's background, icon, title, subtitle, a main button and a second button. Since iOS 26.4 the second button can open a menu of up to three choices, and Apple's own examples use it for "more minutes" options ([Apple Docs: ShieldConfiguration](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/managedsettingsui/shieldconfiguration); [Apple Docs: secondaryButtonSubmenuItems](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/managedsettingsui/shieldconfiguration/secondarybuttonsubmenuitems)). But the shield is a static picture with no text box and no animation ([Apple Forums 838231](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/838231)), so **the "explain your reason" conversation cannot happen on the block screen itself**. It has to happen inside your main app, and getting there is surprisingly awkward. For years Apple offered no supported way for a shield button to open its own app. An Apple engineer warned that apps doing it through hidden ("private") system functions should be rejected in App Review ([Apple Forums 766644](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/766644)). The standard workaround, used by one sec, is a notification: the shield posts a local notification, the user taps it, and your app opens to the right screen. It works only if the user allows notifications, including during Focus modes, and stops iOS from delaying them ([Apple Forums 820790](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/820790); [dev.to, Willheim](https://dev.to/twillywill/the-ios-screen-time-api-in-2026-what-apples-docs-dont-tell-you-151e)). Apple's documentation now lists a new button response, `.openParentalControlsApp`, for iOS 26.5 and later, which would fix this cleanly ([Apple Docs: openParentalControlsApp](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/managedsettings/shieldactionresponse/openparentalcontrolsapp)). However, an Apple support engineer in July 2026 and a developer testing in September 2026 both still described opening the app as unsupported, and nobody has published a working example ([Apple Forums 838231](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/838231); [dev.to, Willheim](https://dev.to/twillywill/the-ios-screen-time-api-in-2026-what-apples-docs-dont-tell-you-151e)). Treat it as promising but unproven until you test it on a real phone.

### Timed unlocks work, with quirks you must design around

Temporary unlocks are well supported. When the user is approved, your app removes the shield and starts a DeviceActivity timer. When the timer fires, a small *extension* puts the shield back; an extension is a mini-program Apple runs separately from your app, even when your app is closed ([Apple Docs: Device Activity](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/deviceactivity); [dev.to, Willheim](https://dev.to/twillywill/the-ios-screen-time-api-in-2026-what-apples-docs-dont-tell-you-151e)). Developers report three timer limits. Calendar schedules shorter than 15 minutes are rejected, at most 20 can run at once, and a short unlock such as "5 minutes" must therefore use a *usage threshold*, meaning "re-lock after 5 minutes of time inside Instagram" ([dev.to, Willheim](https://dev.to/twillywill/the-ios-screen-time-api-in-2026-what-apples-docs-dont-tell-you-151e); [GitHub: react-native-device-activity](https://github.com/kingstinct/react-native-device-activity)). Thresholds count only time the app is on screen, so a 5-minute unlock stretches longer in real time if the user switches away. iOS 26 also brought "phantom thresholds" that fire with no real usage ([Apple Forums 812472](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/812472)). The safe rule is to design every re-lock so that a bug ends an unlock early rather than leaving an app open. The extensions are tiny. The timer extension reportedly gets about 6 MB of memory, and Apple documents that the shield extension cannot use the internet ([Apple Forums 823431](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/823431); [Apple Docs: ShieldConfigurationDataSource](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/managedsettingsui/shieldconfigurationdatasource)). **The AI must run in the main app, not in the blocking machinery.**

Finally, in self-control mode the user always holds the master key. Apple's documentation says that once a person authorizes an app for themselves, iOS lets them delete it as needed ([Apple Docs: requestAuthorization](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/familycontrols/authorizationcenter/requestauthorization%28for:%29.md)). The permission can also be switched off in Settings ([Apple Forums 727291](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/727291?page=2)), and Opal's users have documented a long list of loopholes around its attempts to lock that setting ([Opal Community](https://community.opalapp.com/t/how-do-we-prevent-turning-off-screen-time-access-in-settings-solved-on-ios-26-4/488/50)). Plan for the off switch instead of fighting it. The table below sums up the ground rules as of October 2026.

| What your team wants | Possible on iPhone? | How | The catch |
|---|---|---|---|
| Block Instagram completely | Yes | Screen Time shield on its token | User can revoke permission in Settings |
| Block everything except a few needed apps | Yes | "All except" shield, up to 50 exceptions | A few system apps always stay open |
| Unlock for N minutes, then re-lock | Yes | DeviceActivity timer or usage threshold | 15-minute schedule minimum; iOS 26 timer bugs |
| Let your code (and AI) know which app was picked | No, except for EU users | Opaque tokens | The user must tell the AI what the app is |
| Chat with the user on the block screen | No | Jump to your app via a notification | iOS 26.5 "open app" button still untested |
| Hide Reels inside the real Instagram app | No | Blocked by the sandbox | Only modified, sideloaded copies can |
| Hide Reels on instagram.com | Yes | Your own web view or a Safari extension | Terms-of-service grey area; filters break |
| See what is on screen in other apps | Only during a user-started screen broadcast | ReplayKit / ScreenCaptureKit | Visible indicator; user starts it each time |
| Stop the user from turning your app off | No | Self-authorization is revocable | Detect it and respond instead |

## Ads that promise "no Reels" rely on five workarounds, not magic

Once you know the sandbox rule, the "remove Reels from Instagram" ads stop looking like magic. This research found **no App Store product that edits the native Instagram, TikTok or YouTube app on an ordinary iPhone**. Every product falls into one of five buckets, plus a sixth that lives outside the App Store.

| Technique | Example products | What the user actually gets | The catch |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whole-app blocking (Screen Time and/or Shortcuts) | one sec, Opal, ScreenZen, Jomo, Freedom, AppBlock, Brick | Instagram covered or delayed as a whole | DMs get blocked along with Reels |
| Safari Web Extension | Short Video Hider, SocialFocus, Control Panel for Twitter | Reels/Shorts hidden on websites in Safari | Native apps untouched |
| Built-in browser "wrapper" | Dull, UNDOOMED, SocialLite, Fluid Friction (iPhone) | Instagram's website inside their app, minus Reels | Slower, separate login, weak notifications; no TikTok or Snapchat |
| Shortcuts redirect | ScrollGuard (per its blog), ScrollJail | Tapping the Instagram icon bounces you to a filtered web view | User builds it by hand and can undo it |
| Screen recording plus AI detector | WallHabit, Slowth (open source) | Works with the real app: a Reel appears, you get kicked out | Records the screen nonstop; stops when the phone locks |
| Modified app (outside the App Store) | SCInsta (Instagram), YTLite / uYouEnhanced (YouTube) | The real app with tabs removed | Sideloading, terms violation, account risk |

**Whole-app blockers** are reliable but blunt. AppBlock states that blocking Instagram blocks all of it, not just Reels ([AppBlock](https://appblock.app/can-appblock-block-instagram-reels-youtube-shorts-or-snapchat-stories-on-iphone/)). Opal staff told users the Screen Time API cannot block at the Reels level ([Opal Community](https://community.opal.so/t/i-would-like-to-be-able-to-block-a-feature-of-an-app-but-not-the-whole-app-ie-block-reels-but-not-the-whole-of-instagram/3783)). Brick, a battery-free magnet with an NFC chip that Tom's Guide priced at $59, simply makes you tap the phone against it to unblock apps ([Tom's Guide](https://www.tomsguide.com/phones/brick-review); [Alex Reviews Tech](https://alexreviewstech.com/brick-review-get-your-life-back-from-your-smartphone-maybe/)). One competitor claims Opal uses a VPN profile rather than Screen Time, but that claim is unverified ([Dull vs Opal](https://getdull.app/vs/opal/)).

**Safari Web Extensions** are small add-ons that run code on web pages you visit in Safari. Hiding Reels on a website takes two web languages: *CSS*, which controls how page elements look (including "don't show this"), and *JavaScript*, which can change the page. The extension finds the Reels elements using a *selector*, an address pattern that points to parts of the page's structure (the *DOM*, or document object model), and hides them. Short Video Hider, for example, hides Reels and Shorts on four sites but states that it does nothing to the YouTube app ([App Store: Short Video Hider](https://apps.apple.com/app/id6745738626)). Content blockers, a related Safari feature, do not even run in other apps' built-in browsers, only in Safari and Apple's Safari view ([WebKit Bugzilla 190961](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=190961)).

**Wrapper apps** are the main way "Instagram without Reels" products work on iPhone. Apple's **WKWebView** component lets any app display web pages inside itself, and its `WKUserScript` feature lets the app inject its own script into every page it loads ([Apple Docs: WKUserScript](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/webkit/wkuserscript.md)). Dull loads Instagram in its own browser and strips Reels, Explore and suggested posts before the page draws, while keeping the feed, Stories and DMs. It warns that Instagram's page structure changes every few weeks, so it pushes filter fixes through a remote updater as well as App Store updates ([Dull](https://getdull.app/block-reels-iphone/)). UNDOOMED lists TikTok and Snapchat but cannot enable them because their websites expose too little to filter ([UNDOOMED](https://apps.apple.com/app/id6751837079)). Reviews of these apps complain of slow typing in DMs, failed photo uploads, broken pinch-to-zoom and missing push notifications ([App Store: ScrollGuard](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/scrollguard-block-reels/id6754183872); [UNDOOMED](https://apps.apple.com/app/id6751837079)).

**Shortcuts redirects** use Apple's Shortcuts automation app, which can run a *personal automation* whenever a chosen app opens. one sec has users build one automation per target app, set to run immediately ([one sec help](https://feedback.one-sec.app/help/articles/3470183)). An app cannot create these automations for the user ([Apple Forums 838231](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/838231)), and they reportedly do not fire for about two minutes after the phone is unlocked ([one sec troubleshooting](https://tutorials.one-sec.app/troubleshooting/iphone-or-ipad-app-does-not-work-as-expected)). ScrollGuard's blog describes keeping the real Instagram installed for notifications and redirecting each launch into its own filtered web app ([ScrollGuard](https://scrollguard.app/blog/how-to-block-instagram-reels-iphone)). Its App Store listing, however, describes a Safari extension instead, so the product may offer both.

**Screen-recording detectors** appeared in mid-2026 and are the only products that act while you use the real app. They rely on a **ReplayKit broadcast extension**, the iOS live-streaming feature and the only public way for an app to see pixels from other apps. The user must start it by hand through a system prompt, and a status indicator shows while it runs ([Apple Forums 839450](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/839450)). WallHabit's blog never explains its method, but App Store reviewers say it broadcasts the screen around the clock and stops whenever the phone locks; it rates 3.4 of 5 from 35 ratings ([WallHabit reviews](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/wallhabit-block-shorts-reels/id6751423279?see-all=reviews&platform=iphone)). The open-source Slowth shows the full recipe. A *classifier*, a small machine-learning model that labels each screen frame "short-video screen or not," runs on the phone. It blocks after three positive votes in five frames, then writes a Screen Time shield ([Slowth README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/skar404/slowth/main/README.md)). Note the order: you see the Reel first, then get ejected.

**Modified apps** are the only true removal and they sit outside the App Store. SCInsta hides Instagram's Reels and Explore tabs, but it must be injected into a decrypted copy of Instagram and installed by *sideloading*, meaning installing outside the App Store ([GitHub: SCInsta](https://github.com/Arnvvch/SCInsta)). Sideloaded apps on a free account expire after seven days ([SideStore README](https://github.com/Spidy123222/SideStore/blob/develop/README.md)), and Instagram's terms forbid modifying or reverse-engineering its products ([Instagram Terms](https://www.facebook.com/help/instagram/581066165581870)).

### Network blocking and official APIs cannot single out Reels

A common student hunch is to filter Reels at the network level, using a *VPN* (an app that routes the phone's internet traffic through itself) or DNS blocking (blocking website names). It fails because Reels and ordinary feed media download from the same *CDN* hosts. A CDN, or content delivery network, is the server system that delivers images and video, and Instagram's lives at domains like fbcdn.net, so network tools can block all of Instagram or nothing ([Netify](https://www.netify.ai/resources/domains/fbcdn.net); [Pi-hole forum](https://discourse.pi-hole.net/t/blocking-instagram-on-mobile/15354); [Control D request](https://docs.controld.com/discuss/6a15c6561c9454db81bd18d3)). Apple also restricts the tools themselves. Classic content filters work only on parent-managed child devices or company-*supervised* phones ([Apple TN3134](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/technotes/tn3134-network-extension-provider-deployment.md)). VPN-style apps may be published only by developers enrolled as an organization ([App Review Guideline 5.4](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). The new iOS 26 URL filter matches full web addresses, but it requires hosting a special privacy server and sees only traffic sent through Apple's own web and networking libraries ([Apple Docs: URL filters](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/networkextension/url-filters.md)).

Building your own minimal Instagram is closed off too. Instagram's official API serves only business and creator accounts and offers no home or Reels feed ([Meta: Instagram Platform](https://developers.facebook.com/docs/instagram-platform/overview)), and X's developer terms ban substitute apps outright ([Engadget](https://engadget.com/twitter-new-developer-terms-ban-third-party-clients-211247096.html)). YouTube is the exception. Its Data API plus the official player can power a search-only app, provided your app adds substantial value of its own, leaves the player and ads alone, and lives within a default quota that, as fetched in October 2026, allows **100 searches per day per project** ([YouTube Developer Policies](https://developers.google.com/youtube/terms/developer-policies); [YouTube quota costs](https://developers.google.com/youtube/v3/determine_quota_cost)).

### The platforms' own switches are the only true in-app removal

Ironically, the only real removal of short videos *inside* a native app comes from the platforms themselves. Since April 2026, YouTube users worldwide can reportedly set the Shorts feed limit to zero minutes, though no official YouTube help page confirming adult availability everywhere was found. The block is self-bypassable, with a button to ignore the limit for the day, and Shorts still show up in Subscriptions and from links ([Dataconomy](https://dataconomy.com/2026/04/16/youtube-adds-zero-minute-time-limit-to-shorts-feature/); [Android Headlines](https://www.androidheadlines.com/2026/04/youtube-how-to-block-shorts-new-feature-catch.html)). Turning off watch history also blanks YouTube's recommended home feed ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/16089387?hl=en)). Instagram still has no switch that turns Reels off, and its chronological Following feed reportedly reverts to the recommended feed when the app restarts ([UNILAD Tech](https://www.uniladtech.com/social-media/instagram-opt-out-algorithm-button-to-change-feed-735531-20260911); [Inro](https://www.inro.social/blog/instagram-reels-algorithm-control)). Teens get stronger defaults than adults, such as TikTok's 60-minute limit ([TikTok Newsroom](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/en-us/new-ways-we-are-supporting-parents-and-helping-teens-build-balanced-digital-habits)) and Night Mode under an August 2026 US settlement with Meta ([Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/tech/what-metas-new-teen-restrictions-mean-young-people)). EU users can switch off personalized feeds under the EU's Digital Services Act (DSA) ([Yahoo/Engadget](https://au.news.yahoo.com/eu-digital-act-changing-apps-182430782.html)). Regulators are pushing further. The European Commission preliminarily found TikTok's design addictive in February 2026 and Instagram's and Facebook's in July 2026, suggesting infinite scroll and autoplay be switched off by default ([European Commission: TikTok](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/commission-preliminarily-finds-tiktoks-addictive-design-breach-digital-services-act); [European Commission: Meta](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/commission-preliminarily-finds-addictive-design-instagram-and-facebook-breach-digital-services-act)).

That leaves a simple test for any ad. If you never open the real Instagram app again, it is a wrapper or a Safari extension. If setup asks you to build a Shortcuts automation, it is a redirect. If it asks you to "start broadcast" or share your screen, it is a detector. If the demo video shows an Android phone, the feature may not exist on iPhone at all.

## Research backs a gentle AI gate with a guilt-free exit

### Friction works, and the "never mind" button does the heavy lifting

Your team's idea sits on solid evidence. In a 2023 study published in *PNAS*, new users of the friction app one sec closed the target app instead of opening it in **36% of attempts**. Their opening attempts fell **37%** over six weeks, and actual openings fell **57%** ([Grüning et al., PNAS 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9974409)). The caveats are real: there was no control group, only 280 of 719 recruits stayed all six weeks, and one sec's creator co-authored the paper. The same paper's controlled experiment, with 500 participants in five versions of the app, delivered the most useful design lesson. **The option to back out had the strongest effect.** A 10-second delay added nothing beyond it, and a reflection message on its own did nothing ([PNAS 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9974409)). A Danish government field experiment with **269 teenagers aged 13 to 17** found that interventions at every session cut daily social media activity **31–36%**, that a 6-second wait alone cut it by more than a third, and that teens' satisfaction with social media did not drop ([Danish Competition and Consumer Authority](https://en.kfst.dk/analyses/2025/20250619-disrupting-social-media-habits-a-field-experiment-with-young-danish-consumers)). Friction does wear off. one sec's dismissal rate slid from 43% to about 33% within three weeks ([PNAS 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9974409)). In a larger study of 1,039 users, **26.9% took breaks** from the intervention, usually over weekends, and one user described their brain simply getting used to the wait ([Haliburton et al., CHI 2024](https://www.medien.ifi.lmu.de/pubdb/publications/pub/haliburton2024chi/haliburton2024chi.pdf)).

Stating a purpose adds something friction alone does not. Earlier work found that asking people their intention before opening Facebook, then reminding them of it later, reduced their daily time and visits ([CHI 2024, related work](https://www.medien.ifi.lmu.de/pubdb/publications/pub/haliburton2024chi/haliburton2024chi.pdf)). The closest research analog to your concept is "INA," a desktop AI assistant presented at CHI 2026. The user types an intention, and an LLM (*large language model*, the kind of AI behind chatbots) asks up to two clarifying questions, then watches for off-task behavior. **Clarifying questions produced the largest accuracy gain**, lifting it from 0.805 to 0.871. In a three-week field study with 22 people, INA cut the share of off-task time from 0.166 to 0.104 compared with a simple reminder. Users also rated it more disruptive, and they marked 31.1% of the nudges they rated as wrong ([Choi et al., arXiv/CHI 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.14513v3)). The economics literature explains why a gate helps at all. A randomized study attributed **31% of social media use to self-control problems** that people only partly recognize in themselves ([Allcott, Gentzkow & Song, NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28936)).

### Competitors prove the gate is buildable, not that AI judging works

The commercial landscape confirms feasibility and leaves the interesting question open. Binky Block shows a cartoon chimp coach when you open a blocked app; you argue for access, and it may grant a few minutes or refuse. It is built on Screen Time, receives only opaque tokens, and describes no anti-lying measures ([Binky Block](https://binkyblock.app/)). Overlord, inside the Forfeit app, decides unblocks by cross-checking your claims against your calendar, GPS, Apple Health data and the timestamps and location data of photos you submit as proof ([Overlord](https://overlord.app/)). Accountable AI blocks apps until you submit photo or text proof of a goal ([App Store: Accountable AI](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/accountable-ai-habit-blocker/id6747360923)). None of these has independent reviews or published results. Apart from Binky Block, this research found no product where an AI reads a typed reason and sets the unlock length. **The niche is open, but nobody has shown that AI judging beats a plain 6-second wait.**

### The AI cannot catch a good liar, so your code must hold the limits

An AI cannot verify that "cooking instructions" is true. Self-report prompts are already gamed: a Jomo user admits to typing gibberish or a single letter to get past its intention box ([App Store: Jomo](https://apps.apple.com/lc/app/jomo-screen-time-blocker/id1609960918)). Chatbots can be talked around, too. In June 2026, hackers took over high-profile Instagram accounts simply by asking Meta's AI support chatbot for access ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/06/01/hackers-hijacked-instagram-accounts-by-tricking-meta-ai-support-chatbot-into-granting-access/)). Apple says its on-device model is trained to follow the developer's instructions over the user's text, but warns this is not bulletproof ([WWDC25 session 286](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/286/)). The AI's realistic jobs are therefore narrow: reject vague reasons with one clarifying question, size the unlock to the task (ten minutes for a recipe, not an hour), notice patterns such as a fourth "cooking" unlock in one day, and hand off to the checkup. **Every hard limit belongs in ordinary code, where no amount of persuasion can move it.**

| Part of the unlock decision | Decided by | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose category (task, learning, social, entertainment, boredom, unclear) | AI | "car-building videos" becomes *learning* |
| How specific the reason is (1–5) | AI | "just checking" scores 1 |
| One clarifying question, if needed | AI | "Which dish are you making?" |
| Suggested minutes (capped at, say, 30) | AI | A recipe gets 10 |
| Daily budget, cooldowns, unlocks per hour, school-hours rule | Your code | No more than 45 "ask first" minutes per day |
| Pattern memory | Your code, shown to the AI | "4th cooking unlock today, 34 minutes used" |
| Final lock or unlock | Your code | Clamp the AI's 25 minutes to the 12 left in the budget |

### Apple's on-device AI keeps reasons private and costs nothing

Apple's **Foundation Models** framework, shipped with iOS 26 in September 2025, gives apps a roughly 3-billion-parameter language model that runs on the phone. It is free to use, works offline, and keeps the user's text on the device ([Apple Newsroom](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2025/09/apples-foundation-models-framework-unlocks-new-intelligent-app-experiences/)). It is built for classifying and extracting information, not for world knowledge, which suits an intent judge. Its **guided generation** feature makes the model fill in a Swift data structure you define, so you always get a clean answer like the table above instead of free-form text ([WWDC25 session 286](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/286/)). Two limits matter. Each session holds only 4,096 *tokens* of text in total, counting instructions, conversation and answer format; these are word-pieces, unrelated to Screen Time's app tokens, so conversations must stay short ([Apple TN3193](https://developer.apple.com/tutorials/data/documentation/technotes/tn3193-managing-the-on-device-foundation-model-s-context-window.md)). The model also runs only on Apple Intelligence iPhones, meaning **iPhone 15 Pro or newer** ([TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/tech/apple-intelligence-heres-a-full-list-of-the-iphones-ipads-and-macs-thatll-get-apples-new-ai-powers)). Teammates with older phones need a fallback, such as a pick-list of reasons with fixed rules, or a cloud model.

iOS 27 widens the options. Small apps can use Apple's larger model on its *Private Cloud Compute* servers at no API cost, and a new protocol lets apps plug in cloud models such as Claude or Gemini ([Apple WWDC26 guide](https://developer.apple.com/wwdc26/guides/ios/)). A cloud model is cheap. At Claude Haiku 5.5's list price of $0.10 per million input tokens and $0.50 per million output tokens ([Claude API pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)), a 700-token decision costs about a hundredth of a cent, or roughly seven cents per user per month at 20 unlocks a day. The real costs are elsewhere. You need a small server so your API key never ships inside the app, and Apple has required since November 2025 that apps clearly disclose, and get permission, before sending personal data to a third-party AI ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/11/13/apples-new-app-review-guidelines-clamp-down-on-apps-sharing-personal-data-with-third-party-ai)).

### A checkup that measures time, not content, is the honest default

For native apps, your app can know *when* and *for how long* a blocked app was used, but not *what* the user watched. That is still enough for a meaningful checkup. When the usage threshold fires, re-shield the app and send a check-in notification: "Did you find the recipe? Yes / 5 more minutes / I got distracted" ([Apple Docs: DeviceActivityEvent](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/deviceactivity/deviceactivityevent.md)). one sec already sends a similar reminder about the user's stated intention ([one sec help](https://tutorials.one-sec.app/en/articles/3035138)). Log the gap between intent and time ("you asked for three 10-minute cooking sessions, used 34 minutes, and reported getting distracted twice") and show it in a weekly summary. Count the minutes from your own timer events, because Apple's usage-report extension is sealed and cannot pass its data back to your app ([Apple Docs: DeviceActivityReport](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/deviceactivity/deviceactivityreport)). Inside a filtered web view you control, the checkup gets much richer, because your app knows the exact page address (for instance, whether it contains `/reels/`) and the time spent in each section. Watching the screen itself is possible only through an opt-in broadcast, and even on desktop, about half of INA's users raised privacy concerns about screenshots ([Choi et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.14513v3)).

Two last design rules follow from the evidence. First, check the Screen Time permission every time the app opens, since reports say a revoked permission may not register while the app is in the background ([Apple Forums 747915](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/747915)). Answer a switch-off with an offer of a sanctioned weekend break rather than a guilt trip, because breaks are common and users rebound afterward ([CHI 2024](https://www.medien.ifi.lmu.de/pubdb/publications/pub/haliburton2024chi/haliburton2024chi.pdf)). Second, keep the reason text on the phone and store only the category and minutes. If anyone under 13 might use the app, the amended US children's privacy rule (COPPA), in full effect since April 22, 2026, adds parental-consent and data-security duties ([Davis Polk](https://www.davispolk.com/insights/client-update/ftc-prioritizes-coppa-enforcement-new-compliance-obligations-take-effect)). Limiting testers to ages 13 and up is far simpler.

## Six designs route around the walls instead of through them

The workaround is not to break the sandbox; apps that tried hidden system functions or reverse-engineered clients got pulled. The workaround is to decide *where* the user meets social media. The native app becomes the locked, hard path, and an experience your app controls becomes the easy one. The six designs below stack: the first is the foundation, and the rest are optional layers.

### The Gatekeeper locks whole apps and lets an AI hold the key

This is your team's concept built entirely from Apple-approved parts, and it also answers your teammate's preference for an app-specific approach. At setup, the user sorts apps in Apple's picker into three tiers: **Always open** (Messages, Maps, school apps, never shielded), **Ask the coach** (shielded, with unlocks granted by the AI) and **Never** (shielded, with no unlock button or a 24-hour cooldown). A strict "default-deny" mode can block everything except up to 50 needed apps. Because tokens are opaque, ask the user to type a short label for each "ask the coach" app, such as "YouTube – tutorials only." The user typed that text, so your app can store it next to the token and pass it to the AI. When a shielded app opens, the shield shows text your main app prepared in advance and saved in an *App Group*, a shared storage area your app and its extensions can all read ([dev.to, Willheim](https://dev.to/twillywill/the-ios-screen-time-api-in-2026-what-apples-docs-dont-tell-you-151e)). On iOS 26.4 and later, the shield's three-item menu can offer quick reasons like "Reply to a message" so common cases skip typing ([Apple Docs: secondaryButtonSubmenuItems](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/managedsettingsui/shieldconfiguration/secondarybuttonsubmenuitems)). Tapping the main button sends the user to your app's chat through the notification bridge, or through `.openParentalControlsApp` if it tests out on iOS 26.5. The chat produces a structured decision, your code applies the caps, the shield lifts, and a usage threshold re-locks the app and triggers the check-in.

The tools are Swift and SwiftUI (Apple's programming language and its screen-building toolkit), the three Screen Time frameworks, three extension targets (timer monitor, shield look, shield buttons), and Foundation Models. Legal risk is low, because this is exactly what Apple built the API for. The main obstacles are the publishing-approval wait and the timer bugs described above.

### The Clean Room gives each user a personalized, filtered social feed

This is the honest answer to "there must be a way to personalize which parts are removed." Inside your app, a WKWebView loads instagram.com, facebook.com, x.com, reddit.com or m.youtube.com, and your injected CSS and JavaScript hide whatever each user switches off: the Reels tab, Reels in the feed, Explore, suggested posts, YouTube's Shorts shelves and home recommendations, or X's "For you" tab. Control Panel for Twitter, an MIT-licensed open-source project, already forces X to stay on "Following" ([Greasy Fork](https://greasyfork.org/scripts/387773)). Store your selectors in a JSON data file your app downloads, so you can fix breakages without an app update, as Dull and Slowth do ([Dull](https://getdull.app/block-reels-iphone/); [Slowth README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/skar404/slowth/main/README.md)). Keep that file to plain data rather than code, because Guideline 2.5.2 forbids downloading code that changes an app's features ([App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)).

The Clean Room also unlocks ideas no shield can match. A **task mode** opens YouTube's search results for "car building" instead of the home feed when the AI approves that reason. An **AI-suggested filter profile** can propose settings from the user's stated goal: "you use Instagram for DMs and recipes, so hide Reels and Explore and keep Search?" A **path-aware checkup** can nudge the user the moment a "recipe" session wanders onto a Reels page. Paired with the Gatekeeper, native Instagram moves to the "Never" tier and the Clean Room becomes the allowed path.

The trade-offs are concrete. Instagram username-and-password login reportedly works inside such a view ([Dull](https://getdull.app/block-reels-iphone/)). Your app should never collect the password itself; let users type it into Instagram's real page. Google has blocked its sign-in inside embedded web views since 2021 ([Google Developers Blog](https://developers.googleblog.com/en/upcoming-security-changes-to-googles-oauth-20-authorization-endpoint-in-embedded-webviews/)), so plan on signed-out YouTube or the official YouTube API. TikTok and Snapchat are out. Expect slower typing, no native push notifications and regular filter repairs. Also test early whether shielding instagram.com as a Screen Time web domain blocks your own web view as well, since Jomo says its website blocks apply in all browsers ([Jomo help](https://help.jomo.so/en/article/how-to-block-websites-on-your-iphoneipad-using-jomo-ly7jcr/)).

The legal picture is a genuine grey area. Instagram's and YouTube's terms forbid modifying or altering their services ([Instagram Terms](https://www.facebook.com/help/instagram/581066165581870); [YouTube Terms](https://www.youtube.com/static?template=terms)). Apple's Guideline 5.2.2 requires that you be specifically permitted under a service's terms to display its content ([App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). The Juno app for YouTube was little more than a web view that restyled YouTube's page, and Apple pulled it in October 2024 after YouTube complained ([Christian Selig](https://christianselig.com/2024/10/juno-removed/)). Dull and UNDOOMED survive on the App Store today, apparently because the platforms have not objected rather than because any rule protects them.

### The Detour reroutes the real app's icon into your app

For users who keep tapping the familiar Instagram icon, a Shortcuts automation ("when Instagram opens, open my app's Clean Room") sends them to the filtered version. Your app exposes the action to Shortcuts through an *App Intent*, Apple's way for apps to offer actions to automations ([one sec help](https://feedback.one-sec.app/help/articles/3470183)). Add a "launchpad" of *deep links*, special addresses that open an app straight to one screen. For example, `instagram://direct-inbox` opens Instagram's DMs without passing the feed. These links are undocumented and can break without notice ([DEV Community](https://dev.to/ahandsel/instagram-url-schemes-1k6n); [Zippy](https://blog.zipthe.link/instagram-deep-links-guide)), and your app must declare each scheme it checks for in its settings file ([Nutrient](https://nutrient.io/guides/ios/miscellaneous/youtube-links)). The Detour needs no special Apple approval, but the user must build each automation by hand and can delete it anytime. Once the native app is open you lose control, so pair it with a Gatekeeper timer. Updates can also break automations, as an iOS 26.6 bug did with "app closed" triggers ([Apple Forums 841128](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/841128)).

### The Safari Sidecar reuses your filters in Safari

The same CSS and JavaScript can ship as a Safari Web Extension, so users who open instagram.com in Safari get the same filtered view. Apple requires that extensions request access only to the sites they need ([App Review Guidelines, 4.4.2](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). Good references are Slowth, a complete open-source Xcode project, and the minimal No-YouTube-Shorts extension ([Slowth](https://github.com/skar404/slowth); [GitHub: No-YouTube-Shorts](https://github.com/Guillaume351/No-YouTube-Shorts)). Note that Slowth is GPLv3-licensed, so copying its code would require releasing your whole project under the same open-source license.

### The Spot Check verifies focus sessions with opt-in screen sharing

For approved sessions in native apps, such as 30 minutes of car-building videos in the real YouTube app, the user can opt into a "verified session." It runs as a screen broadcast that samples a shrunken frame every few seconds and passes it to a tiny on-device classifier ("is this a full-screen vertical-video feed?"); if the session drifts off-task, the app re-shields. The constraints are severe. The user must start each broadcast through a system prompt and can stop it from the status bar ([Apple Forums 839450](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/839450)). The extension gets only about **50 MB of memory**, which rules out large models ([ZEGOCLOUD](https://www.zegocloud.com/docs/faq/ios-screen-sharing-crash)). Copy-protected video records as black ([Apple Forums 86521](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/86521)), and Guideline 2.5.14 demands explicit consent and a visible recording indicator ([App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). The ground is also shifting: an Apple engineer says iOS 27 replaces the ReplayKit broadcast with ScreenCaptureKit's sharing picker ([Apple Forums 831465](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/831465)), and whether the new system keeps capturing while your app is in the background is unverified. Google's Gemini Live shows that user-started screen sharing with an AI is acceptable on the App Store ([Tom's Guide](https://tomsguide.com/ai/google-gemini/gemini-live-is-free-for-iphone-users-heres-how-to-share-your-screen-and-camera)). Frame this as a reward ("verified sessions earn longer unlocks") rather than surveillance, and treat it as a stretch goal.

### The Settings Coach turns on the platforms' own switches

The cheapest layer is an onboarding checklist. It walks users through setting YouTube's Shorts limit to zero and pausing watch history, using Instagram's Following feed, setting a TikTok screen-time passcode that a friend holds, and, for EU users, enabling the non-personalized feed options. It carries zero legal risk and delivers the only genuine in-app Shorts removal available. You can also tie it to the Gatekeeper, for example by granting longer YouTube unlocks once the Shorts limit is on.

### Network filters, supervision and hacked clients are dead ends

Several tempting routes fail. **Network filtering** cannot isolate Reels and needs an organization-enrolled developer account. **Supervising a phone** means erasing it and enrolling it with Apple Configurator on a Mac ([Apple Configurator guide](https://support.apple.com/ar-ae/guide/apple-configurator-2/apd9e4f64088/mac)), and Apple limits MDM (*mobile device management*) apps to businesses, schools and governments ([Guideline 5.5](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)), so it works only as a lab demo. **Sideloaded tweaks** violate Instagram's terms and expose users' logins to third-party code. **Hidden system functions** that open your app from the shield risk rejection and account termination ([Apple Forums 766644](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/766644)). **A reverse-engineered Instagram client** is the riskiest of all: The OG App, which did exactly this, was pulled within about a day in 2022, and Meta permanently banned its developers' accounts ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/meta-cracks-down-on-og-app-instagram-client-005949126.html)).

| Design | Removes *parts* of social media? | Covers | Special requirements | Biggest risk | Class demo | App Store |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gatekeeper | No, whole apps | Any app | Paid account; Family Controls approval to publish | Approval wait; user can switch it off | Very feasible | Feasible |
| Clean Room | Yes, on websites | Instagram, Facebook, X, Reddit, YouTube (signed out) | Standard account only | Platform terms; filters break | Very feasible | Feasible but grey |
| Detour | No, reroutes the icon | Any app | User builds the automation | Easy to undo | Very feasible | Feasible |
| Safari Sidecar | Yes, in Safari only | Same sites as Clean Room | Standard account only | Platform terms; narrow reach | Feasible | Feasible |
| Spot Check | Detects and ejects | Anything on screen | Broadcast extension; small ML model | Privacy, battery, manual start | Hard | Uncertain |
| Settings Coach | Yes, via platform switches | Mainly YouTube | None | Switches are dismissible | Trivial | Feasible |

*Feasibility ratings are this report's judgment from the evidence above.*

## A five-phase build gets a demo running before Apple's approval arrives

### Windows can't build iPhone apps, so plan around one Mac and real iPhones

**Xcode**, Apple's app-building program, runs only on macOS, and App Store Connect now rejects apps built with tools older than the iOS 26 SDK ([dev.to, Willheim](https://dev.to/twillywill/the-ios-screen-time-api-in-2026-what-apples-docs-dont-tell-you-151e)). Xcode's *Simulator*, the virtual iPhone on a Mac, is effectively useless here. In iOS 26 testing, the Screen Time permission prompt appeared but never completed, the app picker stayed empty and the timer extension never ran ([dev.to, Willheim](https://dev.to/twillywill/the-ios-screen-time-api-in-2026-what-apples-docs-dont-tell-you-151e)). A free "Personal Team" Apple account does not get the Family Controls capability at all ([Apple Forums 764682](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/764682)). So even a class demo needs a paid Apple Developer Program team, currently $99 a year in the US, and Apple's enrollment terms may require an adult, such as a parent, teacher or the school, to hold the account. Publishing through **TestFlight** (Apple's beta-testing service) or the App Store needs a separate "Family Controls (Distribution)" approval. The team's Account Holder must request it for the app *and* for each extension's *bundle ID*, the unique identifier of each app piece ([Apple Docs: Requesting the entitlement](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/familycontrols/requesting-the-family-controls-entitlement)). Developers report waits from a few weeks to 31–33 days ([Apple Forums 820811](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/820811); [Apple Forums 826743](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/826743)).

| What you need | Why | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| One Mac with Xcode 26 or later | Only way to build and install iOS apps | A school lab, teammate or family Mac; rented cloud Macs make plugging in a test iPhone awkward |
| At least one real iPhone on iOS 16+ (ideally 26.5+) | Screen Time does not work in the Simulator | iPhone 15 Pro or newer to test on-device AI |
| Paid Apple Developer Program team | Free accounts lack Family Controls | Development builds work immediately; no approval needed |
| Family Controls distribution approval for app + 3 extensions | Required for TestFlight and the App Store | File on day one; weeks to a month or more |
| Your Windows PCs | Web filters, AI rubric, server, design | Cannot compile iPhone apps |

The Windows machines still carry real work. Teammates can prototype Clean Room filters by opening instagram.com in a desktop browser's mobile view and writing CSS selectors that hide Reels. They can also write the AI's rubric and test it against 30–50 sample reasons through a cloud AI, and design every screen. For the iPhone code, native Swift and SwiftUI is the best choice: the extensions must be native Swift anyway, and Foundation Models is a Swift framework. React Native with Expo is the most mature cross-platform alternative, through the `react-native-device-activity` library, but it still needs the Mac, and Expo's cloud builds are unavailable until the distribution approval covers every bundle ID ([GitHub: react-native-device-activity](https://github.com/kingstinct/react-native-device-activity)). Flutter's plugins are early-stage ([pub.dev: flutter_family_controls](https://pub.dev/documentation/flutter_family_controls/latest/)).

### Build in this order so each phase is demo-able on its own

| Phase | What you build | Where | You're done when |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Setup (week 1) | Paid team; distribution requests filed for app + 3 extensions; Mac and iPhone lined up; AI rubric drafted and tested on sample reasons | Apple's website; Windows | Requests submitted; rubric sorts 30 sample reasons sensibly |
| 1. Lock and key | SwiftUI app with self-authorization, three-tier picker, custom shield text, a plain "unlock 10 minutes" button, threshold re-lock | Mac + iPhone | Instagram shows your shield, unlocks, and re-locks by itself |
| 2. The coach | Notification bridge to the app (and a test of `.openParentalControlsApp` on iOS 26.5+); chat screen; Foundation Models judge with structured output; hard caps in code; pick-list fallback; a "never mind" button on every screen | Mac + iPhone 15 Pro or newer | Vague reasons get one follow-up; caps hold whatever the user types |
| 3. The checkup | Check-in notification at time-up; intent-vs-time log; weekly summary; permission-revocation detection; sanctioned breaks | Mac + iPhone | Summary shows minutes asked for vs. minutes used |
| 4. The clean room | WKWebView for Instagram and signed-out YouTube; per-user toggles; selectors in a JSON file; page-address tracking feeding the checkup; Shortcuts redirect guide | Filters on Windows; app on Mac | A user hides Reels and Explore and still sends DMs |
| 5. Stretch | Safari extension; settings coach; opt-in spot check | Mac | Optional |

Phases 1 through 3 alone make a complete, defensible class project. They also turn your app into a small experiment: you can compare the AI gate against a plain 6-second wait on yourselves, which no published study has done yet.

### A class demo is safe; an App Store launch adds five more hurdles

For a class demo, the only real requirement is installing development builds from the Mac onto your own phones. Whether other installation routes work without the distribution approval was not verified, so plan on plugging each phone in. An App Store release adds five more hurdles. Beyond the distribution approval covered above, Apple's current guidelines require that the app offer more than a repackaged website (4.2), avoid third-party trademarks without permission, so no "…for Instagram" names or logos (5.2.1), have permission under each platform's terms to display its content, which none grants (5.2.2), and get explicit consent before sending anything to a cloud AI (5.1.2(i)), with separate consent rules for any screen recording (2.5.14) ([App Review Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/)). The enforcement history is a fair warning for the Clean Room. Meta permanently banned the developer of the "Unfollow Everything" wellbeing extension and sent him a cease-and-desist letter in 2021 ([Knight Institute](https://knightcolumbia.org/content/facebook-banned-me-for-life-because-i-help-people-use-it-less)). A 2024 lawsuit seeking a ruling that such tools are legally protected was dismissed as premature, leaving the question unresolved ([Knight Institute](https://knightcolumbia.org/content/federal-judge-says-challenge-in-zuckerman-v-meta-cannot-go-forward-yet)). For a school project the practical risk is low, but it is not zero. Keep platform logos out, never handle passwords, don't block ads, and lead with your original features: the AI coach, the checkup and the summaries.

## Conclusion

The question your team started with, "how do we remove parts of other apps?", turns out to be the wrong one on iPhone. Every successful product answers a different question: how do we make the real app the hard path and a better experience the easy one? Seen that way, the iOS restrictions stop being a wall and become a set of design rules. Screen Time controls *whether and when*, a web view you own controls *what*, and the AI controls *why*. The evidence also suggests your AI's value lies less in policing than in specificity and memory: pushing a vague "just checking" toward a concrete, time-boxed task and noticing patterns over days. The single most effective ingredient in the research is the humble option to back out, so that button should never be more than one tap away.

The most original contribution available to your team is not technical at all. Nobody has published evidence that an AI judge beats a plain countdown, and nobody has shipped a checkup that measures the gap between what users said they would do and what they did. A class project that builds the Gatekeeper, adds the intent-versus-time checkup, and honestly tests whether the AI helps would be doing something the commercial apps have not shown. And because regulators in Europe and the US are pushing platforms to add their own off switches, an app designed as a coordinator of those native controls, rather than a fighter against the sandbox, is positioned to grow more useful over time.
